The Walland the Reef

Coral, fishponds, and the rising sea at Heʻeia, Kāneʻohe Bay, Oʻahu

What protects a loko iʻa as the sea rises, and how do we know?

The sea reaches a fishpond wall in two ways: as still water, which is tide plus sea level rise, and as waves carried on that water. Coral reefs reduce the second and not the first. This study separates the two using public data and a simple wave model, and marks what requires fieldwork and community knowledge.

Summary

  1. Heat. In situ water temperature from Kāneʻohe Bay, 1992 to 2023, gives peak heat stress of 10.3, 13.6, and 16.9 degree heating weeks in 2014, 2015, and 2019: NOAA Alert Levels 2, 3, and 4.
  2. Waves. In a transparent wave model with assumed geometry, a degraded reef at today's sea level delivers more wave height to the wall than a healthy reef under 1.22 m (4 ft) of rise.
  3. Still water. No reef condition changes the still water level. With an assumed 1.00 m wall crest and 0.30 m tide, still water reaches the crest at 0.70 m of rise, below Hawaiʻi's 4 ft planning minimum.
  4. Next step. Each assumption maps to a measurement in the funded project: drone and in water benthic surveys, GPS wall elevations, and pressure sensors across the reef.
0 Positionality and protocol

Scope and position

My training is in African and African American Studies, with minors in marine science and environmental policy. My field experience includes reef ecology in Belize, water sampling and microbial analysis in North Carolina, and a biological oceanography cruise aboard RV Shearwater. Kāneʻohe Bay is Kanaka Maoli water. Heʻeia Fishpond is cared for by Paepae o Heʻeia.

This study uses public data only. It does not map or interpret knowledge held by kiaʻi loko. Where that knowledge would change a result, the text says so. Field data collection would follow community protocol and the direction of partners and project leads.

Two works frame my position. Tiffany Lethabo King uses the shoal to theorize where Black studies and Native studies meet (King 2019). Nitasha Tamar Sharma examines Black belonging in Hawaiʻi in relation to Kanaka Maoli sovereignty (Sharma 2021).

Precedent. Steward, Ninomoto, Kane, Burns, and colleagues used drones, lidar, and in situ water sensors to study king tide flooding at loko iʻa in Keaukaha, Hilo (Steward et al. 2024). Those ponds belong to that partnership and are not mapped here. Heʻeia is used because its public record is extensive.
1 Sea of Islands

Why the place is vulnerable

Land use upslope has shaped the nearshore for about two centuries.

Heʻeia Fishpond is a walled loko kuapā. The stewardship footprint on the map encloses about 34 hectares. Its mākāhā, the sluice gates, let tide and young fish pass. After restoration, two gates on the northeast side carry more than 80 percent of the exchange, and the pond is driven mainly by tide (Möhlenkamp et al. 2019).

The pond and the reef share a watershed. Winter and colleagues describe the moku system, in which land and sea were managed as one social and ecological region (Winter et al. 2018). Select an entry below to locate it on the map.

  1. Six to eight centuries ago

    The kuapā is built (Möhlenkamp et al. 2019).

  2. About the 1840s

    Flooded taro systems, which trapped sediment and nutrients, give way to a plantation economy. Sediment reaches the nearshore and smothers reefs and fishponds (Möhlenkamp et al. 2019).

  3. About 1902

    Red mangrove is introduced to Hawaiian estuaries to hold sediment. It later overgrows fishpond walls and gates (Möhlenkamp et al. 2019).

  4. May 1965

    A flood breaks part of the Heʻeia wall. A mangrove island forms in the breach and stands for decades (UH Mānoa News 2019).

  5. Through the 1970s

    Sewage discharge into the southern bay drives a shift from coral to algae. Diversion follows late in the decade, and the reef begins to recover (Smith et al. 1981; Hunter and Evans 1995; Bahr, Jokiel and Toonen 2015).

  6. 1996 to 2019

    Bleaching is documented in 1996, 2014, and 2015. In July 2014 a freshwater flood kills coral in the northern bay before the heat event (Bahr, Jokiel and Rodgers 2015; Bahr, Rodgers and Jokiel 2017). Heat stress in 2019 exceeds all three (Chapter 2).

  7. 2012 to 2018

    Biocultural restoration removes invasive mangrove and rebuilds gates. Exchange rises and residence time falls (Möhlenkamp et al. 2019).

Reading the arc

Epeli Hauʻofa described Oceania as a sea of islands, connected rather than isolated (Hauʻofa 1994). Candace Fujikane reads Kanaka Maoli cartography as mapping for abundance (Fujikane 2021). Teresia Teaiwa ties Pacific seascapes to militarism and tourism (Teaiwa 1994).

In my Caribbean atlases, plantation economies pushed enslaved provision grounds uphill onto marginal soil. At Heʻeia, sediment from the plantation economy moved downhill onto reef and pond. The reef conditions measured in Chapters 2 and 3 carry that history.

2 Reading Heat

The stress

Three decades of water temperature from inside the bay, converted to heat stress.

Corals bleach when water stays hot for long enough. NOAA Coral Reef Watch counts that stress in degree heating weeks. Each day the water sits at least 1 °C above the local maximum monthly mean, the excess is added up over a rolling 12 weeks and divided by seven (Skirving et al. 2020). NOAA Alert Level 1 begins at 4 and signals risk of reef wide bleaching. Level 2 begins at 8 and adds risk of mortality in heat sensitive corals. Levels 3, 4, and 5, added in 2024, begin at 12, 16, and 20.

The figure below uses instruments inside Kāneʻohe Bay, not satellites: the NOAA station at Moku o Loʻe, the CRIMP buoys, and the Hawaiʻi Institute of Marine Biology weather station, as compiled in a public repository. Heat stress is recomputed here with NOAA's method.

What the record says

Three events cross into mortality territory. Peak stress reached 10.3 degree heating weeks in 2014, 13.6 in 2015, and 16.9 in 2019. By NOAA's current scale that is Alert Level 2, 3, and 4 in successive events inside the same bay. The 2014 peak arrived in October, after the July freshwater kill had already stripped coral from the northern bay (Bahr, Jokiel and Rodgers 2015).

The record has limits. Bleaching occurred in Kāneʻohe Bay in August 1996 (Bahr, Jokiel and Rodgers 2015), yet in situ heat stress that year peaks at 0.15. A fixed threshold did not register that event. Bahr and colleagues report that seasonal timing, as well as the duration and magnitude of heating, affected event severity. Values for 1998 to 2001 come from one station that reads cooler than the others and should be treated with caution.

Relevance to the project. NOAA satellite heat stress uses 5 km pixels. The NOAA regional station for the Main Hawaiian Islands uses a maximum monthly mean of 26.98 °C, 0.43 °C below the in situ value here, and listed Bleaching Warning in its latest data, September 22, 2026. Drone thermal infrared measures water surface temperature only; reef scale thermal maps would need calibration against loggers on the bottom.
3 Ground Truth

How we measure a reef

The same ten meters of reef look completely different at centimeter, three meter, and ten meter pixels.

Burns and colleagues combined structure from motion photogrammetry with GIS to measure reef structure in three dimensions (Burns et al. 2015), and extracted complexity metrics from 3D meshes and elevation models (Fukunaga and Burns 2020). Structure also matters for waves: on the Kāneʻohe barrier reef, bottom friction dissipates most incident wave energy under typical conditions (Lowe et al. 2005).

Resolution

At 10 m, this tile is one Sentinel 2 pixel, and a classifier must assign one class to a mixture. At drone resolution, colonies and bleaching are resolved individually. The project pairs satellite, drone, and in water observations for this reason.

From flight to finding

  1. Fly multispectral and thermal drone surveys at low tide in calm, clear water, with GPS ground control.
  2. Build orthomosaics and elevation models by structure from motion; correct imagery for sun glint and water column effects.
  3. Classify benthic habitat: live coral, bleached coral, algae, sand, rubble.
  4. Test the map against in water photoquadrats. Report a confusion matrix, overall and per class accuracy, and area estimates adjusted for error (Congalton 1991; Olofsson et al. 2014).
  5. Carry coral cover, bleaching, and structural complexity into the wave and flood analysis in Chapter 4.

An accuracy table is a result, so none is shown here. It will come from survey data.

4 The Wall and the Reef

Two kinds of water

Reefs break waves. They do not lower the tide.

Kane and colleagues showed that coastal wetlands on Maui and Oʻahu flood slowly, then rapidly, once sea level passes a critical elevation (Kane et al. 2015). Anderson and colleagues found that adding seasonal wave inundation and chronic erosion to passive flooding, which includes groundwater, exposes up to twice the land (Anderson et al. 2018). At Keaukaha, drone elevation models matched observed king tide flooding while older lidar models overestimated it; kiaʻi loko reported that the areas flagged only by lidar do flood during severe weather and large swells (Steward et al. 2024). Tide alone does not capture that flooding.

Hawaiʻi's 2022 guidance sets 4 ft (1.22 m) of sea level rise as the minimum for all planning and 6 ft (1.83 m) for public infrastructure and other projects with low tolerance for risk. The state's passive flooding maps for those levels exclude wave run up (PacIOOS 2026). The model below addresses that wave term. It moves a wave from the bay edge to the kuapā, removing energy by bottom friction and by breaking in shallow water, and compares the wave crest with the wall. The structural loss slider is shared with Chapter 3.

What the model shows

With default settings, wave height at the wall is 0.18 m for a degraded reef at today's sea level and 0.13 m for a healthy reef under 1.22 m of rise. Harris and colleagues reached a comparable conclusion for reefs in Tahiti and Moorea: in the near term, structural complexity matters more than sea level rise for protection from average waves (Harris et al. 2018).

Still water is independent of reef condition. With the assumed 1.00 m crest and 0.30 m tide, still water reaches the crest at 0.70 m of rise. The reef affects how often and how much a wall is overtopped by waves before that point, not whether still water eventually exceeds it.

Measurements that would replace assumptions: bathymetry and reef flat depth from drone and lidar surveys; friction factors from pressure sensors across the reef; wall crest elevation from GPS survey; groundwater and rainfall flooding. Not modeled: wind waves generated inside the bay, wave direction, setup, and infragravity waves. The 2 m offshore wave height is a demonstration value, not drawn from a wave record.
5 The Reef as Infrastructure

Valuing reef protection

Coral reefs reduce incident wave energy by an average of 97 percent (Ferrario et al. 2014). Beck and colleagues valued this globally as avoided flood damage, comparing present reefs with reefs lowered and smoothed by 1 m across several storm return periods (Beck et al. 2018). Storlazzi and colleagues valued reef flood protection for U.S. states and territories, including Hawaiʻi (Storlazzi et al. 2019).

Both rely on expected annual damage: flood damage weighted by annual probability and summed. The reef's value is the difference between that sum with and without reef protection. The figure uses invented numbers to show the calculation. It is not an estimate for Heʻeia.

What the ledger cannot hold

Damage functions count buildings and infrastructure. They do not count a fishpond's harvest, its use in teaching, or the gatherings it supports. Chan and colleagues describe such values as relational (Chan et al. 2016). A thesis would report the monetary estimate, because agencies use it, alongside the values it omits, described in terms set with the community.

6 Coda

Two oceans, one method

My reef field experience began in Belize. The Mesoamerican Reef and Kāneʻohe Bay differ in species, wave climate, history, and who holds authority over them. The workflow on this page can be applied to both; interpretation cannot be transferred between them.
